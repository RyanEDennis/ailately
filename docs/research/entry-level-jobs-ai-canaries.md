# Research notes: Canaries and Coal Mines: AI and Entry-Level Work (article 42)

## Stanford Digital Economy Lab: "Canaries in the Coal Mine?" (Brynjolfsson, Chandar, Chen)
- Current (revised) version dated Aug. 12, 2026; original release was August 2025 (assignment brief cites a 13-point original relative-decline figure for early-career workers from the Aug. 2025 release; this session's fetch retrieved only the current, revised page).
- As of the Aug. 12, 2026 revision, using ADP high-frequency administrative payroll data covering millions of US workers through June 2026, the paper reports a 19-percentage-point employment gap for young workers aged 22-25 in AI-exposed occupations versus less-exposed peers, up from the original 2025 finding — the gap "widened steadily since August 2025."
- No economy-wide job displacement detected; declines concentrated specifically in occupations where AI substitutes for tasks, while AI-complementary occupations held flat or rising employment.
- Mechanism: the gap opens through reduced hiring, not increased separations; adjustment occurs through employment levels, not wage cuts.
- Authors explicitly frame findings as "early, descriptive indicators — canaries in the coal mine — rather than causal estimates," and note "these patterns attenuate when controlling for education, show some divergent trends predating generative AI."
https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publications/canaries-in-the-coal-mine/

## Challenger, Gray & Christmas job-cut reports (2026)
- July 2026: 33,429 total job cuts announced, the lowest monthly total in two years. AI cited as the leading reason for job cuts for the fifth consecutive month: 10,970 cuts attributed to AI in July alone (33% of the month's total). Year-to-date 2026 AI-attributed cuts: 112,713 (~24% of all 2026 cuts). Cumulative AI-attributed cuts since Challenger began tracking the category in 2023: 184,538.
- Quote, Andy Challenger (chief revenue officer): "Tech remains the center of gravity for this year's cuts, and AI is still the reason companies give."
- Technology led all sectors in AI-attributed cuts: 9,867 in July, 149,023 year-to-date (31% of all 2026 cuts). Example cited: Visa announced a 7% workforce reduction attributed to AI-driven efficiency.
- June 2026: 45,849 total cuts, down 53% from May; AI was the leading cited reason for a fourth consecutive month.
- August 2026 (published Sept. 2, 2026): 52,881 cuts, up 58% from July; restructuring (not AI specifically) led causes that month, ending AI's run atop the list; cumulative Jan-Aug 2026 cuts: 529,914, down 41% year-over-year.
https://www.challengergray.com/blog/challenger-report-layoffs-fall-hiring-picks-up-ai-leads-for-fifth-straight-month/

## NY Fed Liberty Street Economics, "Businesses Are Using AI to Transform Work, Not Cut Jobs" (Sept. 1, 2026, Abel/Deitz/Emanuel/Montalbano)
- AI usage: 61% of service firms use AI (up from 40% in 2025, 25% in 2024); 51% of manufacturers (up from 26% in 2025, 16% in 2024).
- Investment intensity mostly modest: 75% of service firms and 90%+ of manufacturers call AI investment minimal to modest; only ~15% of service firms call it significant, ~5% a major strategic priority.
- Median share of a firm's workers actually using AI, among adopters: 17% (service), 7% (manufacturing).
- Layoffs directly attributed to AI remain uncommon in this survey: 4% of service firms reported AI-related workforce reductions (up from 1% the prior year); 0% of manufacturers reported AI-related layoffs.
- Reduced hiring is more common than layoffs: 15% of service firms said they hired fewer workers than they otherwise would have because of AI; manufacturers saw some reduced hiring versus none previously.
- Increased hiring to support AI implementation: 13% of service firms, 0% of manufacturers.
- Retraining is the primary adjustment mechanism: 33%+ of service firms and 20%+ of manufacturers reported retraining workers (AI literacy, task automation, prompt engineering, responsible use).
- Directly on point: the piece cites other research noting "entry-level workers may be affected significantly, as AI can substitute for routine tasks often performed by newer employees, potentially creating barriers to workforce entry."
https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/09/businesses-are-using-ai-to-transform-work-not-cut-jobs/

## Yale Budget Lab, "Tracking the Impact of AI on the Labor Market"
- Published July 16, 2026; updated Aug. 19, 2026.
- Methodology: synthetic differences-in-differences approach to assess AI-exposure effects.
- Key finding: "The occupational mix is not yet changing in ways that clearly align with the introduction of AI into the workforce," and "measures of AI usage show no connection to changes in employment or unemployment," as of mid-2026.
- Frames itself as an ongoing tracker, committing to regular updates, acknowledging effects could still emerge over a longer horizon.
https://budgetlab.yale.edu/research/tracking-impact-ai-labor-market

## Economic Innovation Group, "Measuring the Economic Effects of AI" (Nathan Goldschlag)
- Published July 2, 2026.
- Core argument: US statistical agencies lack adequate infrastructure to answer basic questions ("How many firms are using Artificial Intelligence? What are they using it for?"), and "without improved measurement, they risk getting the policy response wrong" — a methodological caution against strong causal claims in either direction, rather than a direct empirical rebuttal.
https://eig.org/measuring-ai/

## AI labs' own hiring (context, reused from articles 8 and 9 research already gathered this session)
- Thinking Machines Lab's headcount surpassed 150, more than quadrupling since its 2025 launch, even as a third of its founding team departed (American Bazaar, May 14, 2026) — but hiring at this and comparable frontier labs skews overwhelmingly toward PhD-level researchers and senior engineers commanding six- to nine-figure packages, not entry-level roles. This is characterized in the article as an inference/interpretation, not an independently sourced claim about entry-level hiring specifically at labs.

## Angle
Two credible data traditions disagree on timing more than on direction. Stanford's payroll-level analysis and Challenger's cause-coded layoff tracker both find a specific, escalating AI signature in the labor market, concentrated in tech and in the youngest, most task-substitutable workers. Yale Budget Lab's macro occupational-mix data and the NY Fed's survey-based approach find the aggregate signal much weaker so far, with retraining outpacing layoffs and AI-attributed workforce reductions still a minority even among AI adopters. EIG's contribution is procedural: neither side's instruments are precise enough yet to fully settle the question. The entry-level-specific finding — reduced hiring rather than firing, a "barrier to workforce entry" rather than a mass layoff — is the one place where multiple independent sources (Stanford, NY Fed) actually converge, even though they disagree sharply on how large or economy-wide the effect is.
